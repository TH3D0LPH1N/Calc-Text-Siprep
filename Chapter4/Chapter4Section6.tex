\textbf{\underline{\large{4.6: Graphical Analysis I}}} \par

In the last section, we looked at graphing functions given information regarding their derivative, but now, we will reverse that process. As we already know, there is a significant amount of layering and parallelism between a function and its first and second derivatives. The zeroes and signs of one shed light on the intervals of increasing, decreasing, extreme points, concavity, and/or point(s) of inclusion of another. \par

The interconnectedness between a function, its first derivative, and its second derivative can be summarized thus: \par

        & \signs{$f(t)$ concave up}{POI of $f(t)$}{$f(t)$ concave down}

The relationship here is present upwards. The zeroes of a function refer to the extremes of the one above it and the POIs of the one two levels up. We will explore this same concept with integrals in a later chapter. \par 

Almost any trait can be found from a known first derivative curve, but unfortunately the derivative is not enough to tell the exact original curve. This is because although the critical values and $x$-values of the POI are known, their $y$-values cannot be found. Similarly, the zeroes of the original curve cannot be found from its derivative. \par

Taking a look at the graph and analyzing when the derivative changes sign, we see that we have relative maxima at $\boxed{-3 \text{ and } 7}$, and relative minima at $\boxed{-7 \text{ and } 6}$. \par 
    \vspace{11pt}
    (b) Let's look on our sign pattern to see when the second derivative switches signs. We see that we have a sign switch at $x = 0, 3$, and $5$. Therefore, we can say that $f(x)$ has points of inflection at $\boxed{x = 0, 3, \text{ and } 5}$.
\end{tcolorbox}

Now, let's move on to a practical application of this skill. We will learn how to apply interpreting and using the graph of the first derivative in the context of rectilinear motion. \par 

\begin{tcolorbox}[example]
    \textbf{Ex 4.6.6: } Consider a variation of the graph in \textbf{Ex 4.6.3}. If that graph represents the velocity $v(t)$ of a particle in rectilinear motion, what can be deduced about the acceleration and the distance? \\

\begin{tcolorbox}[solution]
    \textbf{Sol 4.6.6: } Recall that velocity is the derivative of distance, and acceleration is the derivative of velocity. Therefore, we can reinterpret this problem as looking for the behavior of the original function and the second derivative given the first derivative. Let's take a look at the sign patterns that we created in $\textbf{Sol 4.6.3}$: \\
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
            \draw[<->, thick] (0,0) -- (8,0);

            \node at (2,-0.6) {$-9$};
            \node at (4,-0.6) {$-3$};
            \node at (6,-0.6) {$10$};
            \node at (-0.5, -0.6) {$x$};

            \node at (1,0.5) {$-$};
            \node at (2,0.5) {$0$};
            \node at (3,0.5) {$+$};
            \node at (4,0.5) {$0$};
            \node at (5,0.5) {$-$};
            \node at (6,0.5) {$0$};
            \node at (7,0.5) {$+$};
            \node at (-0.5, 0.5) {$f'(x)$};
        \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center} \vspace{11pt}
    \begin{center}
        \begin{tikzpicture}[baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
            \draw[<->, thick] (0,0) -- (6,0);

            \node at (2,-0.6) {$-6$};
            \node at (4,-0.6) {$5$};
            \node at (-0.5, -0.6) {$x$};

            \node at (1,0.5) {$+$};
            \node at (2,0.5) {$0$};
            \node at (3,0.5) {$-$};
            \node at (4,0.5) {$0$};
            \node at (5,0.5) {$+$};
            \node at (-0.5, 0.5) {$f''(x)$};
        \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center} \vspace{11pt}
    Let's begin with the acceleration. We know that a positive second derivative means that the particle is accelerating, and a negative second derivative means that the particle is decelerating. Simply by taking a look at our sign pattern, we can deduce that the particle is \textbf{accelerating to $t = -6$, decelerating from $t = -6$ to $t = 5$, and accelerating again beyond $t = 5$}. \par 
    \vspace{11pt}
    Now, moving on to the distance. From $\textbf{Sol 4.6.3}$, we determined that $s(t)$ has a relative maximum at $t = -3$ and relative minima at $t = -6 \text{ and } 5$. Therefore, we know that the \textbf{distance reaches a local maximum at $t = -3$ and local minimums at $t = -6$ and $t = 5$}. \par
    \begin{tcolorbox}[interesting]
        But, what about the distance of the particle from the origin? What can we say about that? As it turns out, we cannot determine any useful information about the particle's distance from the origin if we are simply given the graph of a higher derivative. This is related to the ``$+C$'' appended to the end of an integral, but we will dive into this in a later section!
    \end{tcolorbox}
\end{tcolorbox}
